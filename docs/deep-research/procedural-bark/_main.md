# Photorealistic Procedural Tree Bark for Games: A Technical Reference Guide (Non-Neural, GPU/WebGPU-Oriented)

## TL;DR
- The strongest non-neural pipeline for asset-quality bark combines a **stress-driven fracture simulation** (Federl & Prusinkiewicz's bi-layered growth model, or Lefebvre & Neyret's semi-empirical "Synthesizing Bark") to lay out the crack macro-structure, **layered procedural noise** (Perlin/Worley/Gabor) for meso- and micro-detail, and **reaction-diffusion** for organic weathering (moss/lichen mottling) — all of which map cleanly onto WebGPU compute shaders using ping-pong storage textures.
- For representation: generate **2D map sets** (albedo, height/displacement, normal, roughness, AO) offline-in-the-browser via compute passes, then render the relief at runtime with **tessellation + displacement** (for silhouettes/close-ups) or **parallax occlusion / relief mapping** (cheaper, no added geometry). True 3D geometry cracking (FEM/mass-spring/DSC) is the most photorealistic for hero assets but is the least WebGPU-friendly and is best baked offline.
- Prioritize, in order of practicality: (1) **noise-composited height-field bark** (à la Substance Designer graphs, fully GPU-parallel, "semi-fast"); (2) **Lefebvre–Neyret strip-based fracture** for controllable, age-varying cracks; (3) **reaction-diffusion + Worley** for weathering and cellular plate patterns; reserve **FEM/mass-spring/DSC growth simulation** for offline baking of the highest-fidelity displacement maps.

## Key Findings
- Bark realism is dominated by **mesostructure** (visible high-frequency relief: furrows, ridges, plates, lenticels), not by the base mesh or the BRDF. Any credible bark method must produce a **height field / displacement** channel, from which normals and AO derive.
- There are two literature lineages: **physics/biology-based fracture simulation** (mass-spring → FEM → deformable simplicial complex, biologically grounded in cambial/secondary growth) and **appearance/procedural** methods (noise, Voronoi/cellular, reaction-diffusion, image-guided). The physics lineage gives the most defensible photorealism and correct topology of cracks; the procedural lineage is dramatically more GPU/WebGPU-friendly and "semi-fast."
- The single most bark-specific, most implementable academic method is **Lefebvre & Neyret, "Synthesizing Bark" (13th EGSR, April 2002, Pisa, pp. 105–116)** — a semi-empirical, interactive-time fracture model on circular growth strips that outputs *either* texture *or* geometry, explicitly designed for the fracture-based bark family.
- **Reaction-diffusion** (Turk 1991; Witkin & Kass 1991) and **cellular/Worley noise** (Worley 1996) are both proven, well-documented, and have ready **WebGPU compute-shader implementations** with public source; they are the workhorses for organic mottling, plate/scale patterns, and weathering.
- Modern GPU displacement rendering (tessellation, parallax occlusion mapping, relief mapping, cone-step mapping) is mature and directly portable to WebGPU's render + compute pipeline, letting you drive bark relief from a generated height map without heavy CPU preprocessing.

## Details

### A. Bark-specific fracture & growth simulation (the core academic lineage)

**1. Federl & Prusinkiewicz — the foundational bark-cracking trilogy (University of Calgary / algorithmicbotany.org)**
This is the seminal body of work on simulating bark cracks from tree growth. All three share one physical model: bark is a **bi-layered material** — an outer dead-bark layer bonded to an inner living tissue layer; the inner layer grows (expanding the trunk circumference) and stretches the outer layer until tensile stress exceeds a threshold and it fractures. This adopts the Skjeltorp–Meakin shrinking-monolayer system.
- **"A texture model for cracked surfaces, with an application to tree bark"** (7th Western Computer Graphics Symposium, 1996, pp. 23–29). *Method:* **mass-spring network** on the growing surface; springs break above a yield threshold and cracks propagate along broken springs. *Known flaws (per Federl's dissertation):* fractures align to the underlying mesh; unclear mapping from simulation parameters to real material properties. *Output:* surface fracture pattern / procedural bark texture (grooves).
- **"Modelling fracture formation in bi-layered materials, with applications to tree bark and drying mud"** (13th Western Computer Graphics Symposium, 2002). *Method:* transitional — presents both the mass-spring approach and a new **finite element method (FEM)**. Surface is seeded with repelling points (Witkin–Heckbert), Delaunay-triangulated, and triangles extruded into **6-node wedge elements**; growth is driven by velocity fields on the inner layer; cracks form by **element removal** and **element splitting** along the plane of maximum principal stress, with adaptive refinement at crack tips.
- **"Finite Element Model of Fracture Formation on Growing Surfaces"** (Computational Science – ICCS 2004, LNCS vol. 3037, pp. 138–145). **DOI: 10.1007/978-3-540-24687-9_18.** The definitive, refined FEM version: wedge finite elements, fracture by node splitting along maximum-principal-stress planes, adaptive mesh refinement + temporary local multi-resolution meshes at crack tips, localized equilibrium recalculation, and adaptive time-step control (advancing simulation to the next fracture event).
- Fullest primary source: **Pavol Federl's PhD dissertation, "Modeling Fracture Formation on Growing Surfaces" (University of Calgary, 2002)**, freely available at algorithmicbotany.org (low-res PDF ~5 MB at algorithmicbotany.org/papers/federl.dis2002.low.pdf, high-res ~40 MB). It documents *why* mass-spring was abandoned for FEM (mesh-alignment artifacts; opaque parameter mapping).
- *Representation:* produces surface crack patterns that can be rendered as displaced 3D grooves or baked to height/texture — so **both** 2D and 3D, but the simulation itself is a serial, iterative FEM/mass-spring solve. *WebGPU implementability:* **Low for the solver** (global sparse stiffness system solved per time step, adaptive remeshing, topological changes — poorly suited to GPU compute; best run **offline** to bake displacement/height maps). *Photorealism:* **very high**; reproduces species-characteristic fissure topology. *Source code:* none published.

**2. Lefebvre & Neyret — "Synthesizing Bark" (13th Eurographics Workshop on Rendering / EGSR, April 2002, Pisa, pp. 105–116)**
- PDF: inria.hal.science/inria-00537488 ; publisher record: diglib.eg.org.
- *Approach:* a **semi-empirical fracture model** targeted at the widespread "fracture-based bark" family. Because tree growth is mostly circumferential, bark is modeled as a set of **circular strips**; fractures appear on a strip, propagate to adjacent strips, and widen over time. Fracture behavior derives from an elastic (Hooke's-law) stiffness model with a fracture criterion; parameters (growth rate, stiffness) are intuitive for artists.
- *Representation:* **both** — explicitly generates *either* geometry *or* texture, and adapts bark features to branch age with continuity across the tree and over time.
- *WebGPU implementability:* **Moderate-to-good.** The authors state in the abstract: *"Our semi-empirical model runs in interactive time, and allows automatic or influenced bark generation with parameters that are intuitive for the artist."* It is far lighter than FEM; the strip/propagation logic is more sequential than pure noise but is tractable and parallelizable per-strip. *Photorealism:* **high** for fissured barks (oak, pine); the best combination of controllability, realism, and cost among the bark-specific academic methods. *This is the single best paper to implement first among the simulation approaches.*

**3. Kratt et al. — "Woodification: User-Controlled Cambial Growth Modeling" (Computer Graphics Forum, vol. 34, issue 2 / Proc. Eurographics 2015; DOI 10.1111/cgf.12566)**
- Authors and affiliations: Kratt, Spicker, Deussen (U. Konstanz); Guayaquil, Fiser, Benes (Purdue); Hart (U. Illinois Urbana-Champaign); Pirk (Esri R&D Center, Switzerland).
- *Approach:* the paper's abstract states it *"present[s] a botanical simulation of secondary (cambial) tree growth coupled to a physical cracking simulation of its bark."* The biological thickening of branches is simulated on a **Deformable Simplicial Complex (DSC)** surface with adaptive subdivision, collision detection, and topological control, extended with temporally coherent texturing and **surface cracking driven by growth stress** via a user-controllable biological model. Includes an algorithm for generating **bark folds**.
- *Representation:* **3D geometry** (evolving mesh) plus coherent texture. *WebGPU implementability:* **Low** — DSC is a CPU-side Lagrangian mesh-tracking method (reference code is GPL C++ at github.com/janba/DSC); best used offline to author hero-asset displacement. *Photorealism:* **high**, biologically grounded; strong for branch junctions and thickening.

**4. Dale, Runions, Hobill & Prusinkiewicz — "Modelling biomechanics of bark patterning in grasstrees" (Annals of Botany, vol. 114, no. 4, 2014, pp. 629–641). DOI: 10.1093/aob/mcu156.**
- *Approach:* the most rigorous **botanical validation** that bark patterns are primarily a **biomechanical fracture phenomenon**. A mass-spring surface model of the trunk undergoes secondary growth; fractures emerge and are **statistically compared to real grasstree images** (matching elongated-to-reticulate pattern variability).
- *Representation:* 3D surface pattern. *WebGPU implementability:* the *model* is offline, but its **findings justify the whole stress-driven approach** and give parameter intuition (compressive growth strains → tensile circumferential/axial stresses → cracking; stiffer/older bark cracks more severely). *Value:* use as the **theoretical grounding** and for tuning crack density vs. age. Free full text at algorithmicbotany.org and PMC (PMC4217681).

**5. Wang, Wang, Liu, Hu & Guo — "Interactive Modeling of Tree Bark" (Pacific Graphics 2003, pp. 83–91; IEEE Xplore doc. 1238250)**
- PDF: cg.cs.tsinghua.edu.cn/papers/pg2003.pdf.
- *Approach:* **appearance/image-based**, not physical. Takes a single bark photo; uses **texton-channel analysis** — the paper states *"For each pixel of I₀, the filter response is a 36-dimensional data vector"* (a bank of 36 Gaussian-derivative filters), clustered by K-means with K≈25 — to segment features (ironbark, vertical/horizontal fracture, tessellation, furrowed cork, lenticel), then assigns a **height field** per channel via similarity-based texture editing, and corrects lighting. *Output:* **textured height field** (2D maps for a bark sample).
- *WebGPU implementability:* filtering/segmentation is a CPU/authoring step, but the **output height-field representation is exactly what you want to feed a WebGPU displacement/relief shader**. *Photorealism:* **high** (image-derived). Good reference for *which bark feature classes to model*.

**6. Crack-pattern simulation methods usable for bark (broader fracture literature)**
- **Gobron & Chiba, "Crack pattern simulation based on 3D surface cellular automata"** (The Visual Computer, vol. 17, no. 5, 2001, pp. 287–309; DOI 10.1007/s003710100099) and **"Simulation of Peeling using 3D-Surface Cellular Automata"** (Pacific Graphics 2001, pp. 338–347). *Approach:* multi-layer **cellular automata** on a surface with a stress field; cracks form and layers peel — explicitly noted as applicable to tree bark peeling. *WebGPU implementability:* **high in principle** — cellular automata are the canonical GPU-parallel, ping-pong compute workload. *Photorealism:* good for crack networks and peeling bark (e.g., birch, plane trees).
- **Iben & O'Brien, "Generating surface crack patterns"** (SCA 2006; Graphical Models vol. 91, 2009, pp. 198–208). *Approach:* initialize a stress field on a surface and propagate cracks, iterating to produce hierarchical patterns; cites bark/mud as targets. *WebGPU:* the stress-field relaxation is grid-like and parallelizable, though crack-front tracking is partly sequential. PDF at graphics.berkeley.edu.
- **Martinet, Galin, Desbenoit & Akkouche, "Procedural Modeling of Cracks and Fractures"** and the related "Modeling cracks and fractures" (The Visual Computer 2005, DOI 10.1007/s00371-005-0317-z): **atlas-based** — edit 2D fracture pattern + profile curves stored per material, map onto the surface, and carve a swept volume. *WebGPU:* the runtime mapping/carving is cheap and GPU-amenable; avoids voxelization/tetrahedralization, enabling very thin cracks. Good middle ground between simulation and pure texturing.
- **Hirota, Tanoue & Kaneko, "Generation of crack patterns with a physical model"** (The Visual Computer, vol. 14, no. 3, 1998, pp. 126–137) — a **multi-layer mass-spring** extension of Federl & Prusinkiewicz for surface cracks.

### B. Procedural noise & pattern synthesis (the GPU-native workhorses)

**7. Reaction-diffusion textures — Turk, "Generating Textures on Arbitrary Surfaces Using Reaction-Diffusion" (SIGGRAPH 1991) and Witkin & Kass, "Reaction-Diffusion Textures" (SIGGRAPH 1991, DOI 10.1145/127719.122750)**
- *Approach:* two-chemical (Turing/Gray-Scott) systems whose diffusion+reaction produces organic spots/stripes/labyrinthine patterns; Witkin & Kass add **anisotropic, non-uniform diffusion** and parameterization compensation. *Representation:* 2D texture and displacement.
- *WebGPU implementability:* **excellent** — the canonical GPU compute pattern (ping-pong storage textures, one Laplacian stencil per texel). Verified public implementations: **robert-leitl/webgpu-reaction-diffusion** (WebGPU compute), the Codrops/Tympanus WebGPU tutorial (May 2024), amandaghassaei's WebGL Gray-Scott shader, and the "WebGPU Unleashed" tutorial chapter. *Bark use:* labyrinthine/mottled patterns for lichen, algal films, and the fine reticulation between bark plates; anisotropic RD aligns patterns along the trunk axis.
- **Sanderson, Kirby, Johnson & Yang, "Advanced Reaction-Diffusion Models for Texture Synthesis"** (Journal of Graphics Tools, vol. 11, no. 3, 2006) — addresses RD's two historical weaknesses (parameter selection difficulty and cost) and provides **CPU and GPU implementations with sample source online**; enables shaped and coupled/oscillating patterns.

**8. Worley, "A Cellular Texture Basis Function" (SIGGRAPH 1996)**
- *Approach:* scatter feature points in a grid; texture value = distance to nth-nearest point (F1, F2…). Produces Voronoi/cellular patterns ideal for **bark plates, scales, and tessellated cork**.
- *WebGPU implementability:* **excellent.** Stefan Gustavson's 2011 GLSL simplification searches only a 2×2 neighborhood instead of 3×3; his own `cellular2x2.glsl` header (MIT, dated 2011-04-19, github.com/stegu/webgl-noise) notes the tradeoff: *"Speeded up by using 2x2 search window instead of 3x3, at the expense of some strong pattern artifacts. F2 is often wrong and has sharp discontinuities. If you need a smooth F2, use the slower 3x3 version."* Performance is competitive with texture-free Perlin/simplex noise: McEwan, Sheets, Gustavson & Richardson, *"Efficient computational noise in GLSL"* (arXiv:1204.1461), report that their functions *"use neither textures nor lookup tables … are implemented in GLSL version 1.20 … compatible with all current GLSL-capable platforms, including OpenGL ES 2.0 and WebGL 1.0. Their performance is on par with previously presented GPU implementations of noise … and they scale well with increasing parallelism"* — the exact property you want for WebGPU. Public code: Gustavson's GLSL notes, ashima/webgl-noise, jushii/WorleyNoise (Unity compute), gertab/Worley-Noise-GPU. *Bark use:* cell/plate layout, ridge segmentation, crackle masks (widen cell boundaries → fissures, as in the Voronoi cartoon-crack approach).

**9. Gabor noise — Lagae, Lefebvre, Drettakis & Dutré, "Procedural Noise using Sparse Gabor Convolution" (ACM TOG 28(3), 2009, DOI 10.1145/1531326.1531360) and Lagae et al., "A Survey of Procedural Noise Functions" (CGF 29(8), 2010)**
- *Approach:* sparse convolution of Gabor kernels giving **accurate spectral control** (orientation, principal frequency, bandwidth), **setup-free surface noise** (no surface parameterization needed — evaluate from 3D point + normal), and **analytic anisotropic filtering** (anti-aliasing). *Bark use:* directional fibrous grain and anisotropic ridging aligned to the trunk axis — exactly the anisotropy plain Perlin lacks. "Gabor noise by example" and "Filtering solid Gabor noise" (2011) list **bark** among target natural textures.
- *WebGPU implementability:* **good** — procedural, point-samplable, no preprocessing; per-sample kernel splatting costs more than Perlin/Worley but is fully parallel and bounded. *Photorealism:* high for anisotropic bark grain; the analytic filtering is a real advantage for minified game views.
- **Perlin/Improved Perlin noise** (GPU Gems 2, ch. 26) remains the base layer for fractal (fBm) elevation; combine octaves for the low-frequency trunk undulation beneath Worley plates and RD mottling.

### C. Displacement / relief rendering at runtime (turning height fields into visible 3D bark)

Once you have a height/displacement map (generated by any method above), WebGPU can render the relief without baked geometry:
- **Hardware tessellation + displacement:** subdivide and push vertices along the normal by the height map; best silhouettes and true geometry for close-up hero trunks, adaptive by distance. (Note: WebGPU has **no fixed-function tessellation stage** — you implement subdivision via **compute-shader mesh generation / mesh-shader-style pipelines** or pre-tessellated patches, which is a real porting consideration versus D3D11/Vulkan.)
- **Parallax Occlusion Mapping (Tatarchuk, 2005)** and **Relief Mapping (Policarpo/Oliveira, 2000/2005)**: ray-march the view ray against the height field in the fragment shader — self-occlusion, self-shadowing, parallax with no added vertices; the pragmatic default for mid-distance bark. **Relaxed/Cone-Step Mapping** (GPU Gems 3, ch. 18) accelerates the search. All are directly implementable in WGSL fragment shaders.
- Derive **normal maps** from the height field by finite differences in a compute pass; derive **AO** cheaply from the height field (e.g., horizon-based or multi-tap) offline-in-browser.

### D. Weathering & aging (moss, lichen, color variation) for the last 10% of realism

- **Desbenoit, Galin & Akkouche, "Simulating and Modeling Lichen Growth"** (Eurographics 2004 / CGF): an **Open-DLA (diffusion-limited aggregation)** propagation model seeding and growing lichen over surfaces, instanced from an atlas. *WebGPU:* DLA aggregation is grid/parallel-amenable; or approximate the *look* with RD + Worley masks driven by curvature/occlusion. PDF at perso.liris.cnrs.fr/egalin.
- **"A survey of aging and weathering phenomena in computer graphics"** (Computers & Graphics, 2008): taxonomy of weathering effects and simulation strategies — the reference map for what to layer (dust, patina, moss, discoloration) and how.
- Practical layering (as in production Substance graphs): drive moss/lichen/dirt masks from **height (cavities catch moisture), slope, and ambient occlusion**, then modulate albedo and roughness; add per-plate color variation via low-frequency noise. This is trivially GPU-parallel.

### E. Practical / industry resources (high quality, implementable)

- **Substance 3D Designer bark breakdowns** — the de facto blueprint for a **fully procedural, non-neural, GPU-friendly height-field bark pipeline** whose node graphs map almost 1:1 onto WebGPU compute passes:
  - Nikola Damjanov's official **Substance Academy "Tree Barks"** series (Adobe) — three methods incl. a **fully procedural** graph and photogrammetry-to-texture baking.
  - Pablo Blanes' "3 Set PBR Wood Bark" breakdown (80.lv) — outputs Base Color, Normal, Roughness, AO, Height; workflow of finding the repeating pattern, base shapes, then microdetail.
  - Peter Sekula's "Creating Cypress Bark in Substance Designer" (80.lv) — height-first workflow with custom Get-Slope / Height-Selector nodes for slope/height-driven masking (directly reproducible as WGSL).
- **Inigo Quilez (iquilezles.org) / Shadertoy** — authoritative, implementable procedural-texturing techniques: value/gradient/Voronoi noise, fBm, domain warping (key for organic bark distortion), analytic filtering of procedural patterns (anti-aliased texturing for free), and SDF/raymarching. Quilez's Pixar work (*Brave*) was procedural bark/foliage. These are WebGL/GLSL and port directly to WGSL.
- **The Book of Shaders (Gustavson/Vivo)** — the clearest practical guide to GPU cellular (Worley) and Perlin noise, with the 2×2 optimization.
- WebGPU noise/compute references: greggman/webgpu-utils, the Google "Game of Life" WebGPU codelab (canonical ping-pong compute), robert-leitl/webgpu-reaction-diffusion.

## Recommendations

**Stage 1 — Ship-fast procedural height-field bark (start here).** Build a WebGPU compute pipeline that composites: (a) fBm Perlin for low-frequency trunk undulation; (b) **Worley/cellular noise** for plate/scale layout and crack masks; (c) **domain warping** (Quilez) to break up regularity; (d) fine Perlin/Gabor for micro-roughness. Output height → derive normal (finite differences) + AO in-shader. Render with **parallax occlusion mapping** at mid distance and **compute-generated tessellation** for hero close-ups. This is fully parallel, "semi-fast," needs no CPU solver, and reaches strong photorealism. Benchmark: if plate/fissure layout looks too regular or too "digital," proceed to Stage 2.

**Stage 2 — Add controllable, biologically-plausible cracks.** Implement **Lefebvre & Neyret's strip-based fracture** ("Synthesizing Bark") to generate the macro-fissure network with age variation and cross-branch continuity, feeding its output into the Stage-1 height composite. Use the **Dale/Prusinkiewicz grasstree findings** to tune crack density vs. trunk age/stiffness. Threshold to escalate: if you need *species-accurate* fissure topology (deep oak furrows, curling birch peel) that the semi-empirical model can't hit.

**Stage 3 — Bake hero-asset displacement offline.** For a small number of showcase trees, run a **FEM (Federl–Prusinkiewicz 2004) or DSC cambial-growth (Kratt 2015)** simulation **offline** (CPU or non-real-time GPU) to bake the highest-fidelity displacement/normal maps, then render them at runtime via tessellation/relief mapping. Do **not** attempt these solvers live in WebGPU.

**Weathering pass (parallel to all stages).** Add a **reaction-diffusion compute pass** (Gray-Scott, ping-pong storage textures — reuse robert-leitl's WebGPU implementation) plus height/slope/AO-driven masks for **moss, lichen, and color variation**. This is cheap, GPU-native, and delivers a disproportionate realism boost.

**What would change these recommendations:** If you later relax the no-neural constraint for *offline* asset baking only, neural material upscaling could augment Stage 3 — but nothing above requires it. If WebGPU adds first-class tessellation/mesh shaders broadly, shift more relief from POM to true displacement.

## Caveats
- **The photorealism-vs-implementability tension is real:** the most photorealistic, physically correct crack topology (FEM, mass-spring, DSC) is the *least* WebGPU-friendly (serial sparse solves, adaptive remeshing, topological changes). The most GPU-friendly methods (noise, RD, cellular automata) need artist tuning to avoid a "procedural" look. The recommended pipeline deliberately uses simulation **offline** and noise/RD **online**.
- **WebGPU has no fixed-function tessellation stage** (unlike D3D11/Vulkan); "tessellation + displacement" must be implemented via compute-based subdivision or pre-tessellated patches — budget for this.
- **Timing/cost numbers for the academic simulation papers are largely unpublished** in the freely available sources (Federl's dissertation documents efficiency *techniques* but not headline wall-clock/mesh-resolution figures); treat "interactive time" (Lefebvre–Neyret) and "real-time" (Woodification editing) as authors' claims for their era's hardware, not guarantees on your target.
- Several key papers are **paywalled or in informal proceedings** (the 1996/2002 Western Computer Graphics Symposium papers have no DOIs; content is best accessed via Federl's free dissertation). Page numbers for the 2002 WCGS paper are inconsistently cited.
- **No official source code** exists for the bark-specific simulation papers (Federl–Prusinkiewicz, Lefebvre–Neyret, Wang et al.); the DSC library (GPL C++) exists but is not the bark application itself. You will be reimplementing. Reaction-diffusion and Worley/Perlin/Gabor noise, by contrast, have abundant public GPU implementations.
- **Substance Designer and Shadertoy resources are non-neural and directly relevant**, but they are craft blueprints rather than peer-reviewed methods; validate photorealism against real bark references per species.
